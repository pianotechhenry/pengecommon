## 1589

57 words.

- **'sblood** (VERIFIED): A minced form of God's blood. Wiktionary notes people swore by Christ's body to dodge the commandment against taking God's name in vain.
- **agony** (PARTLY VERIFIED, 14th century): From Greek agōnia, a contest or struggle, from agōn. The death-struggle sense dates from the 1540s and 'extreme pain' only from c.1600, so in 1589 agony still meant a struggle. The source says contest, not specifically wrestling.
- **bote** (FALSE, Old English (bōt)): Bote is Old English bōt, help, remedy or compensation, and 'to boot' comes straight from it. Better is not its descendant: Wiktionary gives better its own line from Old English betera, and lists bōt among its relatives. They are cousins.
- **charm** (PARTLY VERIFIED): From Old French charme, chant or magic spell, from Latin carmen, song or incantation. The c.1300 and mid-15th-century dates are not on the page.
- **collier** (VERIFIED): Middle English colier, earlier coler, one who makes or sells charcoal, from Old English col. A collier burned wood long before he dug coal, hence Colliers Wood.
- **common** (FALSE): Wiktionary cites the 'ordinary or vulgar' sense in Shakespeare's Henry VI, written in 1591, long before the parliamentary enclosures, so it cannot descend from them. Common itself is Latin communis via Anglo-Norman; the legal sense of common as a right is not tested here.
- **commoner** (PARTLY VERIFIED): Commoner is common plus -er, and common is Latin communis, com- plus munis. Wiktionary traces munis to a root meaning to exchange; it does not give the 'bound by shared obligation' reading, so treat that as interpretation.
- **complexion** (VERIFIED): Middle English complexion meant temperament, from Medieval Latin complexio, constitution: the balance of humours, not skin colour.
- **craft** (VERIFIED): Old English cræft meant strength and skill, from a Germanic word for power, the same one behind German Kraft. Virtue later took over its sense of power.
- **cunning** (PARTLY VERIFIED): From Old English cunnende, knowing how, from cunnan, to know. Wiktionary confirms the knowledge root; the cunning man and the later sneer are not dated.
- **deal** (VERIFIED): Deal is Old English dǣlan, to divide or share out, beside the noun dǣl, a part. German Teil is a cognate and dole is related, so the oldest deal is a share-out, not a bargain.
- **disaster** (PARTLY VERIFIED): Via Middle French from Italian disastro, dis- plus astro, star: ill-starred. The 1590s arrival date is not on the page.
- **dismal** (VERIFIED): From Anglo-Norman dismal, Old French dis mals, the bad days, from Medieval Latin dies mali. The unlucky calendar days came first; the gloom followed.
- **earn** (VERIFIED): Earn is Old English earnian. Wiktionary says the Germanic verb was formed from a noun meaning harvest, so earning began as reaping.
- **elder** (VERIFIED): Old English eldra or ieldra is the comparative of eald, old. An elder is simply an older one.
- **emotion** (PARTLY VERIFIED): From Middle French emotion, from Latin emovere, to move out or stir up. That fits the public-disturbance sense, but the 1650s date is not on the page.
- **enchant** (PARTLY VERIFIED): From Old French enchanter, from Latin incantare, and a twin of incant. Wiktionary does not spell out 'sing upon' or date the senses.
- **essay** (VERIFIED, late 16th century): Essay arrives in the late 16th century from Middle French essai, a trial or attempt, in the sense Montaigne had just given it. Behind it is Latin exagium, a weighing.
- **estovers** (PARTLY VERIFIED, Middle English (estovers)): From Old French estover. Wiktionary glosses it as obligation, close to but not the same as 'that which is necessary'.
- **excruciating** (VERIFIED): From Latin excruciare, ex- plus cruciare, to torture, from crux, cross. Wiktionary quotes excruciate from c.1587–88, so it was current in 1589.
- **extent** (VERIFIED): From Anglo-Norman extente, a valuation of land, from extendre, to stretch out. The writ named in the doc is not on the page, but the valuation sense is.
- **felo de se** (VERIFIED): Law Latin felo de se, a felon of himself: suicide as a crime against oneself.
- **forest** (PARTLY VERIFIED): Forest comes from medieval Latin forestis, perhaps from foris, outside: the wood outside. Wiktionary offers that beside a Germanic origin and says nothing about 'outside the common law'; the legal meaning is history, not the root.
- **forheawan** (PARTLY VERIFIED, Old English): Wiktionary glosses Old English forhēawan as to slaughter or cut down. 'Beyond recovery' is the intensifying for- read into it, not a gloss on the page.
- **galdor** (PARTLY VERIFIED, Old English): Wiktionary gives galdor as a form of Old English ġealdor and galan as to sing or to enchant. The double sense of song and spell is in the verb.
- **gospel** (PARTLY VERIFIED, Old English (godspell)): Gospel is Old English godspell, God plus spell, the message of God. Wiktionary says it was probably reshaped from an earlier good-spell, good news, a translation of Greek euangelion.
- **grammar** (PARTLY VERIFIED): Grammar comes from Old French gramaire, learning, from Latin grammatica and Greek grammatikē, skill in letters. Wiktionary's grimoire entry shows Old French gramaire already meant grammar, a book of spells and a magician. The 12th-century date is not on the page.
- **grimy** (FALSE): Wiktionary derives grimy from grime plus -y, and grime from Middle English grim, soot on the face, from Old English grīma, a mask, probably reinforced by Dutch. Nothing links it to the Grimes family of Croydon colliers: keep that as a story people tell, not an etymology.
- **haunt** (PARTLY VERIFIED): From Old French hanter, to inhabit or frequent, from Old Norse or Old English words meaning to bring home, from the Germanic word for home. A haunt is where you live. The date of the ghostly sense is not on the page.
- **holiday** (PARTLY VERIFIED, Old English (hāligdæġ)): Old English hāligdæġ, holy day or Sabbath. The date of the secular-leisure sense is not on the page.
- **hostile** (VERIFIED): Hostile is Latin hostilis, from hostis, enemy. Wiktionary's Latin entry cites Varro saying hostis once meant a stranger, and its root also gave English guest.
- **humour** (PARTLY VERIFIED): From Latin ūmor, liquid; Wiktionary says the four bodily humours gave the sense mood, and mood narrowed to wit. The h is a folk-etymology from humus. The 1680s date is not on the page.
- **influence** (PARTLY VERIFIED): From Medieval Latin influentia, a flowing in. Wiktionary keeps an astrological sense, a force from the stars shaping a person's character. The ethereal fluid and the dating of the shift to social power are not on the page.
- **Lammas** (VERIFIED, Old English (hlāfmæsse)): Old English hlāfmæsse, loaf-mass, the harvest festival on 1 August.
- **marry** (VERIFIED): The oath marry is the Virgin's name, from Middle English Marie. It has nothing to do with marrying, which comes from Latin maritare.
- **neck verse** (VERIFIED): The verse read to claim benefit of clergy, said to be the first verse of Psalm 51. Wiktionary quotes it from 1619.
- **neighbour** (VERIFIED): Old English nēahġebūr, literally near-dweller: nigh plus ġebūr, a dweller or farmer. There is no warmth in it, only distance.
- **nice** (VERIFIED, pleasant sense from 18th c.): From Old French nice, simple or foolish, from Latin nescius, ignorant. Wiktionary dates 'fastidious' and 'scrupulous' from the 14th century, 'foolish' 14th–17th, and 'pleasant' only from the 18th.
- **nightingale** (VERIFIED, Old English (nihtegale)): Old English nihtegale, night-singer, from night plus gale, to sing; the n before g was added later.
- **passion** (PARTLY VERIFIED): From Latin passio, suffering, from pati, to suffer. The sequence of senses and their dates are not on the page.
- **pencil** (PARTLY VERIFIED, paintbrush sense from 14th c.): Pencil first meant a fine paintbrush, from Old French pincil and Latin penicillum, a little tail; it is not related to pen. The brush sense runs from the 14th century to at least 1603, but the source does not test what a writer would have meant in 1689.
- **Penge** (UNVERIFIABLE): Wiktionary has Penge only as a place, with no etymology. Doc 35 cites Gover, Mawer and Stenton, The Place-Names of Surrey (1934), for pen plus coed; check that book or the Key to English Place-Names.
- **read** (VERIFIED): Old English rǣdan meant to counsel, advise or interpret as well as to read, from a Germanic verb meaning to advise. To read was to make sense of something, not only of letters.
- **record** (VERIFIED): Via Old French recorder from Latin recordari, to call to mind, from re- plus cor, heart. To record was to get something by heart.
- **seed** (PARTLY VERIFIED): Old English sǣd, that which is sown, from a root meaning to sow: confirmed. The row's link to Gothic asans, harvest, is a slip: that belongs to earn, which has a different root.
- **silly** (PARTLY VERIFIED): Old English sǣlig, lucky, and Wiktionary traces the slide lucky, innocent, naive, foolish. It gives no dates, so 'still blessed in 1589' is untested.
- **spell** (PARTLY VERIFIED): Spell the noun is Old English spell, news or story, kept in gospel. Spell the verb came through Anglo-Norman espeler and merged with Old English spellian, to tell. The 1570s and 14th-century dates are not on the page.
- **spirit** (VERIFIED): From Latin spiritus, breath or spirit, from spirare, to breathe. It pushed out Old English gast, which survives as ghost.
- **tally** (PARTLY VERIFIED): From Anglo-Norman tallie, a notched stick recording a debt, from Latin talea, a cut rod. Wiktionary does not call it unrelated to tell; it even invites comparison with the Germanic word for number, so treat 'unrelated' as likely, not settled.
- **telling** (VERIFIED): Tell is Old English tellan, to count or to tell, from a Germanic word for number, and tale shares the root. A bank teller and 'all told' still count.
- **temper** (VERIFIED): From Latin temperare, to divide or mix in due proportion, linked to tempus, the fit season. One word for balancing metal, humours and moods.
- **verse** (VERIFIED): From Latin versus, a line of writing and originally a row or furrow, from vertere, to turn. The ploughman's turn at the end of the furrow is the poet's line-break.
- **villain** (VERIFIED): Villain and villein are doublets from Late Latin villanus, a peasant bound to a villa, via Old French vilein. The moral sense came later.
- **virtue** (VERIFIED, inherent-power sense from 13th c.): Latin virtus, manliness or worth, from vir, man. Wiktionary records the sense 'inherent power or efficacy' from the 13th century, so a herb's virtue was its working force. Virtue also pushed out Old English cræft, which survives as craft.
- **watcher** (VERIFIED): Watch is Old English wæċċan, to stay awake; its cousins German wecken and Swedish väcka mean to wake someone.
- **write** (VERIFIED): Old English wrītan, from a Germanic verb meaning to carve or write, from a root meaning to tear. Writing began as scratching.
- **zounds** (VERIFIED): A minced form of God's wounds, the wounds of the crucifixion.
